Addict Some Plugins #002

The July 2026 Audit

Freebies Won. Bundles Lost.

July arrived carrying the hangover from Make Music Month.

You might think this means your inbox can finally rest. It cannot.

The plugin industry apparently decided that summer was the ideal time to place another “revolutionary” tool directly in front of your face, preferably with a countdown timer, an unrealistic list price and several photographs of vintage equipment the software has never physically met.

Fortunately, this is ASP.

We prefer useful software to marketing theatre.

After filtering duplicated campaigns, unrelated retail, hardware disguised as a plugin opportunity and bundles large enough to require their own storage strategy, July’s conclusion became surprisingly simple:

July rewarded useful freebies, small purchases and disciplined filtering. Large bundles and dramatic discount percentages demanded more caution.

Prices and availability in this audit were checked on July 26, 2026. Some of July’s better offers were extremely brief and had already expired, because apparently buying audio software now requires the reflexes of a competitive gamer.

Download This

Aurora — Schematic Sound

Aurora is a waveform visualizer that had recently become free, pay-what-you-want and open source.

It displays a DJ-style, frequency-coloured waveform directly inside the DAW, separating low, mid and high-frequency information into an RGB display. It is not another compressor, another tape machine or another synthesizer with 3,000 presets called things like Eternal Nebula Sequence.

It is a visual utility.

That makes it unusually useful.

Aurora was available as VST3 for Windows and as VST3/AU for macOS when checked for this audit. Its licensing and activation system had been removed when the project became open source.

ASP verdict: Download it. A lightweight utility that helps you understand a session has a better chance of surviving on your computer than another “must-have” saturation plugin.

Amp Squeezer — Clack.DIGITAL

Amp Squeezer is a free frequency-shaped overdrive with a deliberately simple interface and enough aggression to remind you that distortion does not need a cinematic origin story.

The Heat control moves the harmonic emphasis between lower and higher frequencies, while the dry/wet control allows the original signal to remain intact. The plugin was available in VST3 and LV2 formats for Windows, macOS and Linux when reviewed.

Clack.DIGITAL also deserves credit for making the download genuinely free: no account, no email address and no subscription funnel waiting behind the button.

ASP verdict: Get it. It is dirty, direct and does not attempt to become your lifestyle.

Source →

Vinland Drive — Shadaloo Audio DSP

Vinland Drive appeared as a free/pay-what-you-want tape emulator for Windows and macOS.

Its compact signal path combines saturation, soft compression, high-frequency softening, low-end rounding and subtle machine-style movement. It also includes drift, wow, flutter, crosstalk and noise behaviour without covering the interface in forty-seven controls.

The release checked for this audit supported VST3 on Windows and VST3/AU on macOS.

ASP verdict: Worth downloading, particularly if you want fast tape colour rather than a historical reconstruction of every screw inside a 1973 machine.

Source →

XenRoll — Ankalot

XenRoll is for the people who looked at twelve notes per octave and thought:

This feels unnecessarily restrictive.

It is a free and open-source microtonal piano roll that allows pitch placement with one-cent accuracy. It supports MPE and MTS-ESP workflows and was available as a VST3 plugin for Windows, macOS and Linux.

This is not an essential download for every producer. It is, however, a genuinely unusual tool that expands what a conventional DAW piano roll can do.

ASP verdict: Download it if microtonality, alternative tuning or experimental composition interests you. Everyone else may continue placing notes on the usual twelve rectangles.

Additional July Finds

The July inbox surfaced several additional freebies worth checking as dated campaign snapshots:

  • UJAM, Rhodes and BPB’s UFX LOFI
  • Sonuscore’s Elysion Elements
  • a three-plugin giveaway from Stagecraft
  • Audiolatry’s Apus
  • additional BPB and PartialString freebies

The normal freebie rule still applies:

Claim first. Install later. Keep only what proves useful.

A free plugin can still waste disk space, scanning time and several minutes of your life every time the DAW decides to rebuild its plugin database.

The July Sale Section

This is where reasonable producers become amateur accountants.

A discount is not income.

Buying a $150 plugin for $30 does not mean you earned $120. It means you spent $30.

Still, a few July snapshots deserved attention.

TOTAL VI MAX 2 — The Bundle That Broke the Calculator

IK Multimedia’s TOTAL VI MAX 2 appeared at $79.99 during the July campaign, down from a stated bundle value of $1,499.92—roughly 94% off.

This was either extraordinary value or further evidence that plugin list prices belong in the fiction section.

The bundle contained enough virtual instruments to cover pianos, basses, drums, orchestral sounds, organs, synthesizers and several future identity crises.

For a producer building a first serious instrument library, the value was difficult to dispute.

For someone who already owned equivalent tools, it was approximately 200 GB of confirmation that a large discount can still produce a redundant purchase.

ASP verdict: Excellent for a new setup. Questionable for anyone who cannot name at least five included instruments they will use immediately.

SSL Native Channel Strip 2 — The Deal You Had to Catch

SSL Native Channel Strip 2 briefly fell to $19 around the transition from June into July. The campaign ended in early July, so this is now an example of a genuinely good expired deal rather than an invitation to panic-buy it today.

At that price, it was an easy recommendation for anyone who wanted a complete EQ, dynamics and console-style channel workflow.

At full price, it becomes a normal purchasing decision again.

ASP verdict: Great if you caught it. Do not compensate for missing the deal by buying it later out of emotional damage.

Universal Audio — Apparently Everything Was $39

Universal Audio had apparently decided that $39 was a persuasive number.

Both UAD Sound City Studios and the Empirical Labs EL8 Distressor Compressor appeared at that price during the period reviewed.

Sound City Studios combines room, console and outboard-style processing into a single studio-environment plugin. The Distressor offers a flexible compressor model with saturation modes, parallel processing and adjustable operating level.

These were not random catalogue fillers placed on sale to generate movement. Both can solve clear production problems.

ASP verdict: Sound City for room, ambience and character. Distressor for flexible dynamics and controlled violence. Buy the one that addresses an actual need—not both because the prices match.

Sonnox Oxford Inflator — Still Refusing to Become Irrelevant

Oxford Inflator remains one of the rare plugins that survived years of hype and continued being useful.

It increases perceived density and loudness without behaving like a conventional compressor or limiter. That does not make it magic, despite several producers treating the Effect slider as a religious instrument.

But it does make the plugin fast, recognisable and practical.

ASP verdict: Still one of July’s safest paid recommendations if you needed that specific job done.

Shadow Hills Mastering Compressor Class A

It remained a substantial mastering compressor with multiple compression stages, transformer colour options and enough visual authority to make an unfinished track feel temporarily expensive.

ASP verdict: Strong price for someone who needs that particular sound. Completely unnecessary for someone who already owns fourteen mastering compressors and understands six of them.

The Healthy End of the Price Range

The most defensible paid offers observed in July were often below $15:

  • Boz Digital Labs Manic Compressor — $10
  • UJAM Beatmaker VICE 2 — $9
  • SampleScience instruments — around $2
  • LFOAudio Nina Synth VST — $15

That price range offered enough room for experimentation without turning curiosity into a financial plan.

Cheap does not automatically mean worthwhile.

But cheap, specific and immediately useful is considerably healthier than a giant bundle purchased because the discount percentage looked emotionally supportive.

Evaluate Slowly

Some July offers were interesting without becoming automatic recommendations:

  • Eventide Omnipressor — appeared at $49
  • Arturia Rev OCEAN — appeared at $39
  • Klevgrand Summer Sale
  • Acustica Audio intro prices
  • UVI voucher campaigns

These were worth checking only when they solved a specific problem already on the list.

Bitwig Studio 6 and the Summer of Modulation

Bitwig’s Summer Sale offered 25% off Bitwig Studio licences through August 4, 2026.

Bitwig Studio 6 continued to make the strongest case for producers who enjoy modular sound design, flexible modulation and workflows that move comfortably between linear arrangement and clip-based experimentation.

It is not the correct DAW for everyone.

But if your current DAW feels like a spreadsheet that reluctantly learned to play audio, Bitwig remains one of the more interesting alternatives.

ASP verdict: Try the demo before buying. Changing DAWs is not a plugin purchase. It is a temporary change of citizenship.

AI With a Job Description

Yes, artificial intelligence is still here.

No, it has not yet learned when to stop appearing in product descriptions.

The more useful direction is not “generate an entire song while I watch.” It is using machine learning for focused signal-processing problems.

Baby Audio’s TAIP is a reasonable example. The developer used neural-network techniques to model tape-machine behaviour, then placed that model inside a conventional and controllable audio processor.

Humanoid takes a different route, using phase-vocoding and resynthesis for aggressive tuning and synthetic vocal transformation. It is less about replacing the musician and more about deliberately making the musician sound like a transmission from an unstable future.

ASP verdict: AI becomes interesting when it disappears behind a useful control. If the technology is more prominent than the result, the marketing department may be the plugin’s most advanced component.

The ASP $100 Test

Around the turn of the month, an unusually sensible $100 setup briefly existed:

  • Melodyne 5 Essential — $25
  • SSL Native Channel Strip 2 — $19
  • Sonnox Oxford Inflator — $39

Total: $83.

The lesson is not that you missed your chance and should now buy three different products to recover emotionally.

The lesson is that a good $100 budget should solve three clear problems:

  1. editing;
  2. mixing;
  3. finalising.

For the July audit, one safer reading was:

  • treat dated prices as snapshots;
  • download useful freebies before installing everything;
  • buy only tools that solve a current session problem.
The ASP Reality Check

Where will I use this during the next 30 days?

Not someday.

Not after you begin that cinematic project you have mentioned since 2022.

Not when you finally “learn mastering properly.”

In an actual session, on an actual track, within the next month.

July’s inbox contained plenty of unrelated retail, hardware-only offers, educational newsletters, duplicated campaigns and expensive bundles incorrectly classified as plugin opportunities. The filtering mattered as much as the deals themselves.

If you cannot name where you will use it during the next 30 days, do not buy it.

If you cannot describe the immediate use case, the product is not solving a problem.

It is becoming part of your digital furniture.

What Was Ignored

The audit deliberately filtered out several categories that looked noisy, unrelated or too vague to recommend:

  • unrelated retail;
  • political or journalistic newsletters;
  • hardware without relevant software;
  • courses and educational content classified as plugin deals;
  • expensive bundles without an immediate need;
  • offers with confusing price extraction;
  • duplicated or nearly duplicated emails.

Download

  • UFX LOFI
  • Elysion Elements

Buy with a clear purpose

  • Sonnox Oxford Inflator
  • UAD EL8 Distressor
  • UAD Sound City Studios
  • Bitwig Studio 6
  • Shadow Hills Mastering Compressor Class A

Ignore or avoid

  • unrelated retail
  • hardware-only promotions
  • educational newsletters mixed into plugin deals
  • giant bundles without a plan
  • expired offers chased through FOMO
  • discounts louder than the product

Final Verdict

July was a good month for people who knew how to filter.

The winners were not necessarily the companies with the largest banners or the most dramatic list prices. They were the developers offering useful freeware, small tools with clear jobs and discounts that reduced the cost of a planned purchase instead of inventing a new one.

July rewarded people who claimed carefully, bought little and ignored the noise.

Download Aurora.

Try Amp Squeezer.

Keep Vital installed, because it still embarrasses a surprising number of paid synthesizers.

Then close the store tab and make something.

The best plugin deal remains the one that ends with a finished track.

See you next month.
