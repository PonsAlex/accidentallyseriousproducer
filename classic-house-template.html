Breaking the 8-Bar Loop: a 224-bar house arrangement.

Most house projects die inside an eight-bar loop. Every idea gets stacked on top of every other idea, the frequency spectrum fills up, and the arrangement never starts because there is nowhere left to go.

This is the other direction: write the full 100% version of the track first, then duplicate it across the timeline and erase your way down to the arrangement. Subtracting from a finished block is faster than building up from nothing, and it keeps every section related to the same idea.

Below is the whole method — the map, the matrix, the transitions, the automations and the checklist. It targets FL Studio, but nothing in it depends on FL Studio. At the end there is a template you can open and work from.

Start from the climax, not from bar one

The instinct is to build the intro, then the first drop, then figure out the rest. That is also the order in which you run out of decisions. Instead, build the densest section — Drop 1B, bars 81–112 — with every element playing at once. That block is the thesis: if it does not hold up with all thirteen tracks running, no amount of arranging will save it.

Then select the whole block, duplicate it across all 224 bars, and delete clips away according to the matrix below. You are sculpting from a finished block rather than inventing something for every gap.

Read the empty space, not the dots. Only two sections run the full set, and the break is doing more arranging work than either drop: it removes the kick and the sub entirely so the return hits harder without a single extra decibel.

Groove, events and fills

The kick holds the room; everything else moves around it. Five things matter more than the rest:

  • Kick — four attacks per bar, one on each beat. Do not let the bass transients fight it.
  • Clap — beats 2 and 4. Extra layers thicken the attack as long as they stay clear.
  • Closed hats — eighths or sixteenths with alternating velocities, light to moderate swing.
  • Open hats — offbeats, and not necessarily every one of them.
  • Bass — short syncopated notes with gaps for the kick; change the phrase every 8 or 16 bars.

The automations

Balance and frequency

The low end decides whether the track sounds professional. Give each element a pocket and stop moving them around:

  • 20–90 Hz — sub-bass, strict mono. Anything wide down here disappears in a club.
  • 90–250 Hz — kick transient, phase-aligned with the sub. Notch 50–80 Hz on the bass where the kick dominates.
  • 250 Hz–5 kHz — chords, claps, vocals. Carve rather than boost.
  • 5–20 kHz — hats and pads, panned wide. This is where the stereo width comes from.

The checklist

  1. Set the project up. 124 BPM, 4/4, thirteen named Playlist tracks and ten time markers across 224 bars.
  2. Build the rhythm skeleton. Kick, clap on 2 and 4, closed and open hats with light swing. Listen to it without harmony.
  3. Write the thesis. Piano inversions, syncopated bass, organ stabs and vocal chops inside Drop 1B.
  4. Distribute and subtract. Duplicate the block across the timeline, then delete down to the matrix.
  5. Draw the automations. LPF cycles, HPF ramps, reverb swells and sidechain, with the start and end of every clip checked.
  6. Program the fills. Variation every 8 or 16 bars, micro-breaks at the turnarounds.
  7. Review the mix. Sub in mono, no clipping, effect tails, space for the clap.
  8. Listen to all 224 bars. Fix the transitions you can hear, not the ones you planned.

The arrangement is finished when the whole thing plays without unexpected gaps, the two drops have their own identity, the break offers real contrast, the automations move the parameters they are assigned to, and the outro allows a clean exit.

Before you trust the automations

The automation clips exist, they are assigned to real parameters, and they move — but they are generic. The sidechain ducking is the clearest case: it reacts to the kick as its source, and how much it ducks depends entirely on what you load into the other tracks. A hard kick and a dense bass need different numbers than a soft kick and a sparse one.

Treat every value above as a starting point and tune it by ear. Automation that is routed correctly but tuned wrong sounds worse than none, because you stop hearing the problem and start trusting the clip.
