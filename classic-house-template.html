Why this exists

Most house projects die inside an eight-bar loop. Every idea gets stacked on top of every other idea, the frequency spectrum fills up, and the arrangement never starts because there is nowhere left to go.

The template pushes the opposite direction: write the full 100% version first, then duplicate it across the timeline and erase your way down to the arrangement. Subtracting from a finished block is faster than building up from nothing, and it keeps every section related to the same idea.

What is in the project

Twenty-four named Playlist tracks, thirteen of them musical elements — kick, clap, closed and open hats, shaker, congas, bass, piano, organ, pads, vocal chops, FX and fills — plus eleven automation rows for the HPF builds, the reverb swells, the LPF cycles on piano, organ and pads, and the sidechain ducking.

Before you trust the automations

The automation clips exist, they are assigned to real parameters, and they move — but they are generic. The sidechain ducking is the clearest case: it reacts to the kick as its source, and how much it ducks depends entirely on what you load into the other tracks. A hard kick and a dense bass need different numbers than a soft kick and a sparse one.

Treat every value as a starting point and tune it by ear: 3–5 dB on the bass and 1–3 dB on the chords are deliberately mild, the LPF cycles assume a bright piano patch, and the builds only need the filter to travel as far as the material requires. Automation that is routed correctly but tuned wrong sounds worse than none, because you stop hearing the problem and start trusting the clip.

What it does not contain

No samples, no plugins, no MIDI notes. The patterns are empty placeholders named after their track, and the automation clips exist with a flat default envelope for you to draw. If a pattern looks empty, that is correct — it is a slot, not a loop.
